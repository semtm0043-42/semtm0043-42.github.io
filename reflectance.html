Build the reflectance model
one effect at a time.

The robot has five downward-facing surface reflectance sensors. Each sensor has its own gain and offset; the remaining effects are shared characteristics of the sensing system. Your task is to construct an evidence-backed explanation of how surfaces become readings, then test whether it predicts a new observation.

Before writing any code, find the physical locations of the surface reflectance sensors on the underside of the robot. An image of the sensor is placed below. These sensors are very small, about 3x3mm. You should be able to locate 5 of these sensors. Find the white writing that labels them DN1, DN2, DN3, DN4, DN5. If you're having trouble, you can review the official documentation.

The black cuboid above is only part of the total sensor device. The black cuboid is the optical transducer element. A transducer is the element that converts one form of energy into another. In this case, the two small raised black bumps are the transmitter and receiver of infra-red (IR) light.

The transmitter is a light emitting diode (LED) that emits light at the IR frequency. The receiver in this device is a phototransistor. A phototransistor is a type of semiconductor device. The amount of electrical current that flows through the phototransistor depends on the amount of infrared (IR) light that falls on it. In the simplest terms, we can think of the phototransistor as being like a water tap (faucet, or valve) - the more IR light, the more the water can flow (current). The sensor is intended to operate by transmitting light and measuring the light reflected from a surface. Because this sensor transmits energy and measures the response, it is classed as an active sensor.

If there is no reflected light, the sensor will be making a measurement of any ambient light that might be present (i.e., not the light that was transmitted). The black plastic body of the transducer is most likely an optical filter specifically for IR light. Whilst this filter is present, there may still be external sources of IR light that could cause interference (i.e., modify the signal). Examples of strong ambient sources of IR light are the sun or candles because they emit a broad spectrum of light frequencies. Modern lighting such as LEDs typically have a very narrow range of emitted frequencies (not IR).

For this course, it is simply important to recognise that the performance of these sensors depend significantly on physical and environmental factors. Your task is to measure the performance of these sensors for your specific platform - the Pololu 3Pi+ robot. Pololu does not provide an exact part number for the transducer. However, you can review the datasheet of a likely substitute. This datasheet provides some insight into how the performance can vary, and what characteristics can be described. In your work, you will also need to consider further characteristics as they relate to a task or application that you set your robot for Phase 2.

The rest of the sensor electronic circuit is made up of a capacitor and resistor network. In the simplest terms, a capacitor can be thought of as a water tank. To take a reading, the robot first charges each sensor output and then releases it. It measures the time (in microseconds) that the output takes to discharge. Stronger reflected IR makes the phototransistor conduct more, so the output discharges more quickly; weaker reflected IR produces a longer discharge time. For more information on the electronic principles, you can review the application note provided by Pololu.

For now, we need to understand how to read the sensors from the StampC3, and then physically explore and understand their basic operational characteristics.

In Controller.h, find the call:

robot.getSurfaceSensors();

Next, find the definition of this function in Robot.h. Its return type is void, so the function does not return a sensor reading directly. Instead, it requests the latest surface-sensor snapshot and stores a cached copy locally on the StampC3. This copy remains available until the next call refreshes it.

In Robot.h, find the declaration:

RobotSurface_t surface;

This declares a public data member named surface, whose type is RobotSurface_t. The RobotSurface_t type is defined in i2c_datatypes.h. It contains a timestamp_us field and an array called reading, which holds five numeric sensor values.

Returning to Controller.h, find a line where one of these cached readings is used by the line-following controller. Notice the syntax, for example:

robot.surface.reading[0]

Each dot (.) can be understood as accessing something within the preceding object. For example, reading the code from right-to-left: reading[0] is inside surface, and surface is inside robot.

You may also not be familiar with an array construct. Reviewing RobotSurface_t in i2c_datatypes.h, we can see that reading is declared as an array length 5. In C/C++, an array is indexed (accessed) from 0 upwards - in this case, to 4 - the set 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 is five elements in total, corresponding to the five surface reflectance sensors on the robot.

The data values of reading[...] are not voltages or direct percentages of surface reflectance. The sensor electronics first charge a capacitor (a bit like a mini-battery), then release it and measure how long it takes to discharge. The reported values are discharge times in microseconds (µs): stronger reflected IR generally produces a shorter discharge time, while weaker reflected IR produces a longer discharge time. Pololu describes this RC-type measurement process in its 3pi+ 32U4 line-sensor guide and QTR RC-type sensor application note.

Offset

25–35 min

When reading a sensor, we might expect the sensor reading to have a minimum value of 0 and increase over a range of values. In practice, many sensors exhibit an offset bias. An offset bias is a systematic error that shifts each sequential sensor reading by a consistent amount. In an ideal world, two identical sensors observing the same controlled surface would report the same baseline after the experiment is repeated. In practice, optical alignment, phototransistor characteristics, board electronics, and the physical geometry of each sensor location will cause a different offset bias per sensor.

You should anticipate that each of the five sensors will exhibit its own offset. In the context of these sensors, it may help to consider the offset we measure as the "minimum reading value" for each sensor. In the next exercise, investigating Saturation could be understood as establishing the "maximum reading value". In either case, the minimum reading we can establish by measurement will be influenced by environment and physical factors. It is therefore useful to consider the offset we are trying to establish as the reference-condition baseline. Our reference-condition will be the surface we select to measure the sensor against against.

Your task in this exercise is to collect data from the robot via the StampC3, establish an individual reference-condition baseline for each sensor, and determine the characteristics of the differences between them. These measured baselines provide evidence of any channel-to-channel bias or differences. You are encouraged to identify and question assumptions as you work.

Saturation

25–35 min

A sensor saturates when a change in the physical input no longer produces a corresponding change in the reported reading. For the surface reflectance sensors, we can think of this as the maximum possible value. However, it is important to understand that the saturation occurs when the sensor cannot possibly achieve a higher value, whereas the maximum could be used to simply describe the largest value within a range of operation conditions. If the operation conditions changed a previously recorded maximum value could change. Saturation therefore has a specific meaning.

Saturation can be caused by various changes to the sensor input. The input might be the reflectivity of the surface, the distance between the sensor and surface, or infrared illumination. When we discuss the saturation effect on the resultant measurement signal, this is referred to as clipping. For example, if using a more reflective surface does not cause a change in the sensor reading, the sensor must be at saturation and the signal is clipped. Once the electronics or phototransistor is at its limit, different physical conditions can become indistinguishable.

Gain

25–35 min

Gain describes the scale of a sensor’s response: how much the reading changes when the physical input changes. A high-gain sensor is more sensitive over its usable range. However, sensitivity is not the same as accuracy or precision - a sensitive sensor is not necessarily "better". Sensitivity, or a gain factor, can also reduce the effective range of a sensors readings.

To explain this further, it is useful to explore a technical context typical to microcontroller systems. Imagine we are using a sensor to measure a voltage. Our sensor has a configurable gain which provides a continuous transformation from input to output. The microcontroller will then sample the sensor output through an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) to create a digital (discrete) representation of the voltage. Suppose our voltage sensor converts the input \(x\) to an output \(y\), approximately as:

\[y=Gx\]

where \(G\) is the sensor's gain. A larger \(G\) means a small change in the input produces a larger change in the sensor output. This is what is meant by more sensitive. For example:

\[G = 1.0, x = 0.1 \rightarrow y = 0.1\\ G = 5.0, x = 0.1 \rightarrow y = 0.5\\ \]

Suppose that our microcontroller can only represent sensor readings across the voltage range 0 to 3.3 Volts. With a low gain ( \(G=1.0\)), and assuming the sensor will be exposed to an input in the range 0 to 3.3 volts:

\[y = x \]

the sensor could theoretically map all input values to an output across the digital representation range 0 to 3.3 volts. With a gain of 5:

\[ y = 5x \]
\[x = \frac{3.3}{5} = 0.66 \]
\[G = 5.0, x = 0.66 \rightarrow y = 3.30\\ G = 5.0, x = 0.67 \rightarrow y = 3.35 \\ G = 5.0, x = 0.80 \rightarrow y = 4.00\\ \]

Therefore, any input values above 0.66 volts will generate an output that is beyond the representation range of 3.3 Volts. This would mean the effect of the \(G=5\) would be to saturate the sensor with any input larger than 0.66 volts - the signal would be clipped at measurement. In this way, the gain or sensitivity of a sensor can impact the effective range of measurements.

A high gain value may be valuable in some circumstances. For example, if we knew that within a full input range (e.g., 0 to 3.3 Volts) we are expecting to only measure a much smaller range of values (e.g. 0 to 1.5 volts). In which case, a higher gain serves to map a smaller sensor input range (0 to 1.5 Volts) to the fuller 0 to 3.3 Volt measurement range.

Accuracy and precision are specialist terms applied to other characteristics of measurements. If our sensor conversion included an offset value:

\[y = Gx + b\]

The offset \(b\) would mean the sensor output was always "off target" from the true input value \(x\). How close a measured sensor reading is to the true value is the accuracy. Typically, accuracy can be mitigated by calibration: employing a process to identify the systematic error and then remove it from future readings.

If we took successive sensor readings under the same conditions and discovered that the sensor output varies each time, this variation would relate to the sensor's precision. When discussing sensor performance in robotics, precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements, rather than the statistical definition of precision as reciprocal variance. Standard deviation is a useful way to quantify sensor precision because it expresses the spread of repeated measurements in the same units as the sensor readings. A smaller standard deviation therefore indicates greater precision. Importantly, the precision of a sensor can vary independently of its gain.

Signal noise that lowers precision can be regarded as a random error. To mitigate the effect of random error, a filter can be designed to reduce the influence of measurement-to-measurement variation, for example by combining information from successive readings. More sophisticated filters can use a statistical model of the expected measurement variance to determine how much confidence to place in each new reading (for example, the Kalman Filter).

In the following exercises, you will need to decide a set of measurements to take across this range of surface conditions. The following exercise steps will guide you with further considerations to make.

Fitting a straight line turns the idea of gain into a model that makes a prediction. For a surface condition \(x_i\), the fitted line predicts a sensor reading \(\hat{y}_i\). The difference between the measured reading \(y_i\) and that prediction \(\hat{y}_i\) is called a residual:

\(r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\)

For example, if the measured reading is 820 and the line predicts 800, the residual is +20 in the same units as the sensor reading. A negative residual means that the model predicted a reading larger than the one measured. The residual is not a corrected sensor value, and it should not automatically be interpreted as noise: it contains whatever the fitted model has not explained, including measurement-to-measurement variation, curvature in the response, changes in the physical conditions, or limitations in how the printed surface condition represents the sensor input.

Figures 4a and 4b illustrate why residuals are useful here. The response curve can make a straight-line fit look plausible within the selected region, particularly when the raw readings span a large vertical range. Plotting the residuals places a perfect prediction on a common zero line, making the size, direction, and pattern of the prediction errors easier to see. Small residuals scattered around zero would support using the fitted gain over that region. A curve, a run of residuals on the same side of zero, or errors that grow towards either end would indicate that the straight-line model is missing repeatable structure.

A residual pattern does not establish its cause by itself. Saturation, a non-linear response, surface-printing variation, illumination, sensor height, and placement could produce similar evidence. Repeated measurements under controlled conditions and predictions for held-back surface conditions are therefore needed before deciding where the linear gain is a useful approximation. A power-law model is one plausible way to represent a response whose sensitivity changes across a bounded range. Its exponent is commonly called gamma (\(\gamma\)). When both input and output are normalised between 0 and 1, its simplest form is:

\[ \hat{v}=u^\gamma \]
where :
  • \(\hat{v}\) is our predicted sensor response,
  • \(u\) is the surface expected to be provided in the range \([0:1]\).
  • When \(\gamma=1\), the power law has a linear shape; values of \(\gamma\) above or below 1 bend the response in different directions.

To keep things simple, this exercise will guide you to normalise your surface condition values from [0:255] to [0:1], explained a bit further on.

In this exercise you are also guided to explore a second-order polynomial. A second-order polynomial can be used to model a relationship that has a consistent curvature and therefore cannot be adequately represented by a straight line. We can consider a second-order polynomial to be a natural extension of the linear fit applied in exercise 4:

\( \begin{aligned} y &= x && \text{zero-order}\\ y &= bx + c && \text{first-order (linear})\\ y &= ax^2 + bx + c && \text{second-order (quadratic})\\ \end{aligned} \)

Where:

  • \(a\) determines the curvature of the relationship.
  • \(b\) contributes to its linear trend.
  • \(c\) is the intercept.

Graphically, a second-order polynomial produces a parabola. However, over a limited region we might only observe part of that parabola, producing a gently curving relationship suitable to model our sensor, rather than the familiar complete U-shaped or inverted-U-shaped curve.

For example, min–max normalisation commonly maps \(x\) from an original range \([x_{min}, x_{max}]\) on to \([0,1]\):

\[x_{norm} = \frac{x - x_{min}}{ x_{max} - x_{min}} \]

As a further example, a normalisation to \([-1,1]\), this can instead be:

\[x_{norm} = 2\frac{x - x_{min}}{ x_{max} - x_{min}} - 1 \]

But you could equally normalise in to \([0,100]\), \([−100,100]\), or some application-specific range. It is important to note that normalisation and standardisation are sometimes used inconsistently across disciplines. For example, in statistics and machine learning, standardisation often specifically means transforming data to zero mean and unit standard deviation. Therefore, if we are reporting normalisation, it is important to qualify the range.

Characterising measurement noise

30–40 min

Goal: review the measurements collected in earlier exercises, decide whether they provide robust evidence about noise, and characterise the observed variation by comparing uniform and Gaussian candidate models.

When a sensor is held over the same surface, at the same height and under the same lighting, we might expect it to report exactly the same reading each time. In practice, successive readings will often vary. This measurement-to-measurement variation is commonly described as noise.

Noise is a form of random error: it changes the precision of repeated measurements, rather than producing the consistent shift associated with an offset. A noisy sensor can therefore have the correct average response whilst still giving an uncertain individual reading.

In robotics, noise matters in the context of making autonomous decisions. For example, a line-following controller may compare a sensor reading against a threshold. If noise moves successive readings from one side of that threshold to the other, the robot may rapidly alternate its motor commands even though its physical position has barely changed. Filtering several readings can reduce this variation, but filtering can also delay the response to a real change, referred to as lag. A useful noise model helps us judge how much confidence to place in a reading and whether a proposed filter or decision threshold is appropriate.

To investigate noise, we first need to describe variation relative to the typical reading for a fixed condition. The difference between an individual reading and a stated typical value, such as the mean or median, is called a residual. If a sensor has a typical reading of 1000 and reports 1007, its residual is +7. If the next reading is 994, its residual is −6. Examining many residuals allows us to ask whether small errors are more common than large errors, whether positive and negative errors are balanced, and whether the variation appears to have a reliable limit.

A uniform distribution describes a bounded range in which values are approximately equally likely. For example, a uniform residual model from −10 to +10 would treat a residual near 0 as no more likely than a residual near +9, whilst residuals outside the stated limits would not be expected. In the context of a Digital Twin simulation, uniform noise can be a useful simple model for a bounded source of uncertainty, for example by deliberately adding a random value drawn from a uniform distribution to a simulated sensor reading.

A Gaussian distribution, sometimes called a normal distribution, has a different character. Residuals close to its centre are most likely, and progressively larger positive or negative residuals become less likely, producing the familiar symmetric bell shape. We can interpret this bell shape to mean "small noise values are most frequent, and large noise values are infrequent". The frequency of either large or small noise values is determined by the spread of the bell curve.

The equation for a Gaussian distribution is:

\( f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \)

where:

  • \(x\) is the variable being measured.
  • \(\mu\) is the mean, which determines the centre of the distribution
  • \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, which determines the spread.
  • \(\sigma^2\) is the variance.

A Gaussian can be a reasonable approximation when many small and approximately independent influences add together—for example, small electronic, timing, illumination, and mechanical variations. In this exercise, you should review your data collected for your sensors to determine whether a uniform, Gaussian, or neither are appropriate noise models.

Measuring the sensor spatial footprint

35–45 min

Goal: find out how much of the surface contributes to one sensor reading, then check whether your description predicts readings at positions you deliberately kept aside.

It is possible to assume that the surface reflectance sensor reads one tiny point directly beneath it, a bit like a laser beam. In practice, the emitter illuminates an area and the phototransistor receives light returned from an area.

In this exercise, we will attempt to measure the surface area that is provided reflected IR light back to the sensor. This is quite an open-ended task that could be done with increasing levels of sophistication. This exercise will guide you in the simplest approach, which you could choose to expand further.

To keep things simple, this exercise recommends that you measure the performance of the sensor against black surface conditions with a rectangular shape. Different width of rectangles are suggested, with some provided on the A3 test sheet. Whilst it might seem intuitive to use different sized dots, it would be tricky to ensure good alignment of the sensor over the dot. By using long rectangles, with varied width, we reduce the dimensionality of the task from 2D to 1D.

The general principle of this experiment will be to assume a relationship exists between the sensor measurements, the surface, and the field of view. We will incrementally move the sensor from left-to-right across a long rectangle. Consider:

  • If a black rectangle is wider than the sensor field of view, then we should be able to observe a maximum value from the sensor across multiple positions across the rectangle. This is because, in many positions, an entirely black surface fills the sensor field of view.
  • If a black rectangle is narrower than the sensor field of view, then the sensor will observe a partially white reading, and so report a value for all positions across the rectangle that are less than the known maximum value for a black surface.
  • For a black rectangle that is approximately the same width as the sensor field of view, we should be able to observe a distinctive peak sensor reading that matches the known maximum value for a black surface - as the sensor is moved laterally the reading value will sharply fall.

Investigation brief

  1. Start simple: Choose one sensor to work with. Start with a sensor that will offer you the best consistency in terms of placing the robot consistently on the surface.
  2. Avoid assumptions: For the sensor you have selected, recapture the minimum and maximum sensor readings across the black and white surfaces you will be using for this experiment. Note that, you want large enough black and white surfaces that they will definitely be large than the anticipated sensor field of view.
    1. Why might this step be advised? What may have changed?
  3. Design a placement method you can repeat. Make or adapt a target with measured dimensions and registration marks. For this experiment, you want to be able to consistently move and reposition either the robot or the rectangle at fixed intervals.
  4. Collect a spatial profile. Drawing on your experience of the previous exercises, decide on a CSV format for your measurements to include data labels for the rectangle width, the trial number, and so on. Once you have decided your CSV reporting format, capture a full set of measurements for a single rectangle width before proceeding further. Use this first data set to validate your general method.
  5. Turn the profile into an estimate. Plot raw or normalised response against the robot position. For this rectangle width, do you observe either a plateau in the response (flat region) or a peak?
  6. Repeat the above steps for a full set of rectangle widths. The A3 test sheet has some rectangles, you may choose to use more or less, including designing your own. Collect a full dataset across different rectangle widths
  7. Plot your dataset, keeping the response for each rectangle width on the same plot.
  8. Make a spatial prediction. Based on your sensor responses, try to estimate the field of view of your sensor, and so predict where under the sensor you should be able to detect the edge of a rectangle. There may be two approaches (or more) to constructing your estimate. You can use both of the following suggestions, and see if they agree with each other:
    • Using the peak response, the field of view width must be approximately equivalent to the rectangle width.
    • Using a plateau in response, the field of view width must have a relation to the distance between the edge of the plateau response and the known rectangle width.
  9. Verify: Attempt to verify your estimated field of view by placing the edge of rectangle at where you predict it can first be detected - you should be able to observe a change in the sensor response.
  10. Repeat these steps for all 5 sensors to determine if they all behave equally.
  11. Going further: You can progress this experiment further in several ways, for example, by checking if the field of view appears to be symmetrical in the second dimension.
